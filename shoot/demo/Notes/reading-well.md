# Reading well

Most of what we read at work is not written to be read. It is written to be
correct, or complete, or approved, and reading it well is left to the reader.
These are notes on doing that part better.

## Read the shape first

Before reading a long document from the top, look at its shape. The headings
say what the author thought the parts were; the length of each part says what
they actually spent their time on. When the two disagree, the disagreement is
usually the most interesting thing in the document.

A contents list is a picture of that shape. Glance at it before the first
paragraph, and again whenever you lose your place.

## Read once for the argument

The first pass is for the argument, not the details. Read the first sentence
of each paragraph and the last paragraph of each section. If the argument
holds together at that altitude, the details are worth your time. If it does
not, no amount of detail will rescue it, and you have saved yourself an hour.

## Read again for what changed

Documents that matter are rarely finished. A plan is revised after the review,
a spec after the prototype, a note after the meeting. The second reading is
often more important than the first, and it is usually done badly: either the
whole document is read again, or the reader guesses what moved.

Neither is good enough. What a returning reader needs is the difference
between the version they read and the version in front of them, shown on the
page they already know rather than as a list of edited lines.

## Mark what matters

A highlight is a promise to your future self that this sentence will matter
again. Keep the promise small. A page that is half highlighted says nothing
about which half.

The note beside the highlight is where the value is. The highlight says
*here*; the note says *why*. A week later, only the note will remember.

## Keep one thing in view

Long documents are read one block at a time, but everything else on the
screen competes for the same attention: the sidebar, the toolbar, the other
paragraphs. Dimming what you are not reading is not a gimmick. It is how a
page on paper already works — your hand covers the rest.

## Know how long it will take

Before settling into a long document, it helps to know what you are
committing to. Midway through, it helps to know whether you can finish before
the next meeting. A scrollbar says how far down the page you are, not how much
reading is left: a page of code reads at a very different pace from a page of
prose.

## Ask about the text, beside the text

Sometimes the document is in a language you read slowly, or the part you need
is buried in the middle. Asking a model about it is reasonable. Copying it out
to a chat and reading the answer somewhere else is not: the answer loses the
text it was about, and you lose your place.

The better shape is an answer that stands where the text stands — a
translation in the paragraph's place, a note beside the sentence it is about,
a summary you can open and close without leaving the page.

## Follow the links, then come back

A good document points elsewhere: to the decision it depends on, to the
definition of a term, to the section further down that makes the argument
concrete. Following every link loses the thread; following none leaves holes
in your understanding. The useful habit is to look before you leap — see
what the link points at, decide whether it matters now, and only then go.

When you do go, go knowing the way back. A reader who cannot return to the
exact place they left will stop following links, and a document read without
its references is only half read.

## Know what points here

The links a document makes are visible on its surface. The links made to it
are not, and they are often more telling: the plan that depends on this spec,
the note that disagrees with it, the review that already asked the question
you are about to ask. Before editing or arguing with a document, find out
what else leans on it.

## Read at your own measure

Line length, line height and typeface are not decoration. A measure that is
too long makes the eye lose its place on the return; one that is too short
breaks every phrase in two. Text in Japanese and text in English want
different measures, because a full-width character is twice the width of a
Latin letter. Whoever reads for hours should be allowed to set the page the
way their eyes prefer, rather than the way the stylesheet assumed.

## Stop when you are done

Not every document deserves to be finished. The point of reading is to know
what you needed to know. When you know it, close the window.
